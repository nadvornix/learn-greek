How to Pronounce Greek

Greek has been spoken for over 3,000 years, and its pronunciation has changed quite a bit. When you learn Greek today, you'll encounter two main systems: Classical and Modern.

The audio button in this app uses Modern Greek pronunciation, since that's what speech synthesizers support.

The Big Differences

The main thing that changed? Vowels collapsed. Ancient Greek had distinct sounds for η, ι, υ, ει, οι, and υι. Modern Greek pronounces all of these as "ee" (like in "feet"). This is called iotacism.

Classical (Reconstructed)

η = long "e" (like "hair")

υ = French "u" or German "ü"

οι = "oy" (like "boy")

ει = "ay" (like "day")

Modern

η = "ee"

υ = "ee"

οι = "ee"

ει = "ee"

Diacritical Marks (The Squiggles)

Ancient Greek texts are covered in little marks above the letters. Here's what they mean:

Breathing Marks

Every word starting with a vowel has one of these:

ἀ smooth breathing — no "h" sound
ἁ rough breathing — add "h" before the vowel

This is why we write "Hades" ( Ἅιδης — rough breathing) but "Athena" (Ἀθηνᾶ — smooth breathing, no H).

Words starting with ρ (rho) always take rough breathing: ῥυθμός (rhythmos → rhythm).

Accent Marks

These show which syllable to stress and (in Classical Greek) the pitch pattern:

ά acute — pitch rises (or just stress in Modern)
ὰ grave — replaces acute at end of phrase
ᾶ circumflex — pitch rises then falls

In Modern Greek, all three just indicate stress — the pitch distinctions are gone.

Other Marks

ᾳ ῃ ῳ iota subscript — a tiny iota written below. Originally pronounced, now silent.
ϊ ϋ diaeresis — pronounce this vowel separately (not as a diphthong)
